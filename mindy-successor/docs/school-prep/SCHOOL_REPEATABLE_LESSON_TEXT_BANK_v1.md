## Literacy & Numbers — Mara Fen

### Variant A — The Missing Line
Mara slides a short ledger across the desk with one total deliberately omitted. “Don’t hunt for the answer. Show me which line makes the rest impossible.” She waits while you trace the entries, then taps the margin when you find the contradiction. “Good. Numbers are often less about calculation than about noticing where a story stops making sense.”

### Variant B — Plain Words
A cramped notice is pinned to the board. Mara asks you to rewrite it so a tired worker can understand it on the first reading. When you finish, she crosses out one decorative phrase. “Clarity is not the enemy of intelligence. If the reader has to admire the sentence before understanding it, the sentence is working for itself.”

### Variant C — Market Arithmetic
Mara lays out three imaginary purchases and a deliberately awkward budget. “You may keep the elegant solution if you can afford it.” By the end, the arithmetic is simple; the harder part was deciding what mattered first. She smiles faintly. “Useful numbers always belong to a decision.”

## Arcana — Ilyra Venn

### Variant A — Observe First
Ilyra places a faintly glowing sigil under glass. “Tell me what it does.” When you begin with theory, she raises one finger. “No. Tell me what you observed.” Only after you list the light, heat, rhythm, and decay does she allow speculation. “Certainty should arrive last.”

### Variant B — Controlled Error
A practice pattern is drawn with one intentional flaw. Ilyra asks you to predict the failure before she activates it. The result is small and harmless, but not quite what you expected. “Excellent,” she says, already writing notes. “An error that surprises us is more educational than a success we never understood.”

### Variant C — Competing Explanations
Ilyra gives you two plausible explanations for the same magical effect and refuses to say which she prefers. You spend the lesson designing a way to distinguish them. When your proposed test isolates one variable, she nods. “That is the difference between having an opinion and having a method.”

## Rhetoric & Social — Cassian Reed

### Variant A — Same Point, New Room
Cassian asks you to make the same argument three times: once to a friend, once to a suspicious stranger, and once to a bored official. After the third attempt he laughs. “There. The point survived, but the doorway changed. Most bad persuasion is just the right sentence sent to the wrong room.”

### Variant B — Listen for the Gap
Cassian tells a short story and asks you to interrupt whenever you think you can improve his argument. The first two times he waves you off. On the third, you wait long enough to hear the assumption he never stated. “Exactly. People reveal more in the gap they expect you to fill.”

### Variant C — Recover the Awkward Moment
He deliberately gives you an embarrassing opening line and orders you to recover without pretending it never happened. You acknowledge it, redirect, and earn an approving clap. “Charm is not avoiding every stumble. It is making the stumble look like part of the route.”

## Etiquette & Presentation — Eliane Marrow

### Variant A — The Entrance
Eliane has you enter the same room repeatedly. She changes only one detail each time: your pace, where you look, when you speak. “People decide whether you are comfortable before they decide whether they like you,” she says. “Give them fewer reasons to wonder.”

### Variant B — Three Forks, One Principle
A formal place setting appears more complicated than it is. Eliane lets you study it, then removes half the pieces. “Rules are easier when you understand what problem they solve.” The lesson becomes less about memorizing objects and more about making other people feel at ease.

### Variant C — Deliberate Presentation
Eliane adjusts a sleeve, then steps back. “Now tell me what you meant to communicate.” When your answer does not match the impression, she has you change one detail rather than everything. “Presentation is editing. Panic is redesign.”

## Field Craft — Rowan Pike

### Variant A — What Changed?
Rowan leads you across a small practice yard and then turns you around. “What changed since we entered?” A gate is open, a rope moved, one footprint is fresher than the rest. Rowan says nothing until you finish. “You don’t need sharper eyes. You need to stop deciding what is unimportant before you see it.”

### Variant B — One Tool Short
A simple field task is laid out with one expected tool missing. Rowan refuses to replace it. You test two awkward substitutes before finding a workable method. “Good. Equipment matters. Dependence matters more.”

### Variant C — Choose the Route
Three paths cross the practice ground: shortest, safest, and easiest to retreat from. Rowan asks which is correct. When you say it depends on the goal, he nods once. “Exactly. Routes are decisions, not answers.”

## Physical Training — Tovan Kest

### Variant A — Repeatable Form
Tovan stops you halfway through a difficult drill. “You can finish badly, or restart well.” The second attempt is slower but controlled. He nods. “Strength you cannot repeat is a performance. We are training something you can use tomorrow.”

### Variant B — Pace
He sets a steady rhythm and refuses to let you sprint the easy portion. The final repetitions are uncomfortable but manageable. “Most people spend tomorrow’s effort in the first minute,” Tovan says. “Keep something in reserve until you know the whole distance.”

### Variant C — Coordination Before Force
Tovan gives you a weighted pole and asks for a clean sequence rather than maximum effort. Each correction makes the movement feel lighter. “Power is expensive. Alignment is cheap. Learn which one solves the problem first.”

## Arts & Performance — Nessa Lark

### Variant A — Commit to the Choice
Nessa stops a rehearsal because your gesture is halfway between two ideas. “Wrong is usable. Unclear is boring.” She asks you to choose one intention and exaggerate it until the room understands. Only then does she help you make it subtle again.

### Variant B — Change the Rhythm
A familiar phrase is repeated at three different tempos. Each version feels like a different scene. Nessa grins when you notice. “Good. Meaning is not only the words or the movement. Timing tells the audience what to expect from them.”

### Variant C — Borrow, Then Transform
Nessa gives you a simple pattern from an older performance and asks you to change one element without losing its structure. Your first version is imitation; the second starts to sound like yours. “Tradition is material,” she says. “Use it. Don’t hide behind it.”

## Body Confidence — Sera Vey

### Variant A — Posture Without Pose
Sera has you stand naturally, then asks you to notice where you are holding unnecessary tension. A small adjustment changes how steady you feel. “Confidence is not freezing into an impressive shape. It is knowing you can move without apologizing for taking space.”

### Variant B — Deliberate Care
A grooming exercise becomes a lesson in sequence and attention rather than decoration. Sera asks which steps actually make you feel more prepared. “Keep what serves you. A routine should support your day, not become another audience you have to satisfy.”

### Variant C — Boundaries in Presentation
Sera gives you three presentation choices and asks which one feels intentional rather than merely expected. When you choose, she asks you to defend it in one sentence. “Good. Confidence is easier when the choice is actually yours.”
